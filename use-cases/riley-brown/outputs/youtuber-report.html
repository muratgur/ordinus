Creator profile

Riley Brown is a 29-year-old creator based in San Francisco who started on TikTok the day ChatGPT launched in December 2022. His first video earned 20 million views and took him from zero to 200,000 followers in less than two weeks — a start that set the pattern for everything since: first in, faster than anyone else, before the category had a name.

He named that category himself. "The first vibe coder" is his self-description, and it has held. The premise is simple: non-developers — founders, marketers, product managers, anyone with an idea and no engineering background — can build real mobile and web apps using AI tools and plain language. Riley demonstrates that premise on camera, typically for 22 minutes at a stretch, once a week, for an audience of 251,000 YouTube subscribers and 1.5 million followers across six platforms.

He is also the co-founder of VibeCode, an AI mobile app builder that has raised $9 million in seed funding, serves 10,000-plus paid users, and has generated more than 40,000 apps. Backers include Alexis Ohanian's Seven Seven Six fund, Point Nine Capital, and eFounders. VibeCode is not a side project — it is the other half of his identity, and the relationship between his content business and his startup is the most strategically interesting question in his portfolio right now.

What you are doing right

You named the category, built a YouTube identity around it, and then founded a startup to build the actual infrastructure that vibe coders use. No peer in the AI education space can make that claim. Matt Wolfe curates AI news. Cole Medin teaches coding agents. Liam Ottley runs communities. You build the thing you teach — a moat that none of them can replicate from their current position.

Your YouTube momentum is acceleration, not a plateau

Plus 22.4% subscriber growth and plus 35% view growth in a single 30-day window is not a channel that has run out of gas. These are numbers that suggest the audience for what you are building has not yet been fully captured — people are still discovering the channel for the first time, not just loyal viewers repeating watch sessions. The direction is right and the velocity is increasing.

A 2.9% engagement rate tells a specific story

Industry averages for YouTube channels in the 250,000-subscriber range typically sit between 1% and 2%. Your 2.9% rate, combined with 161 average comments per video, signals an audience that is not passive. These are people who form opinions about what they watch and say so publicly. That reflects an audience that trusts your judgment, not just your access to trending tools — and that quality is genuinely difficult to manufacture.

You have refused to sound like a machine

Your public commitment to writing without AI assistance — "the more you use AI for writing your scripts, the more you're going to sound like AI, and in the long run, sounding like AI is suicide" — is a differentiator that will compound in value as the space becomes noisier. In a category saturated with AI-voiced tutorials and template-derived hooks, a human voice that says something original is an increasingly scarce asset. You understood this before it became obvious to everyone else.

Managing seven X accounts with a combined 200,000-plus followers, reposting viral content across all of them weekly — you have reported this strategy tripled VibeCode's revenue in two months. Most creators running a single account at ten times your X following have not thought to operate this way. Your next chapter

These three moves come from what the data actually supports — not a generic creator playbook, but actions that address specific gaps against specific assets you already have. They are ordered by resource cost: the first requires a decision this week and no new production; the third requires coordinating two organizations.

Move one: install the funnel that the infrastructure already supports

The Discord exists. The Substack exists. The Maven "Vibecoding 101 Guide" free PDF exists. What does not exist is a consistent, prominent path from 1.5 million social followers to any of these assets. A single newsletter opt-in — with the Maven guide as the lead magnet — added to every YouTube video description, the channel banner, the Instagram bio, the X profile bio, and the rileybrown.xyz home page above the fold would begin closing a gap that has been widening for years. This is not a content decision or a product decision. It is a promotion decision, and it costs almost nothing to make.

The ceiling here is demonstrably real: Liam Ottley has 311,000 Skool members in a structurally similar niche. Matt Wolfe has 230,000 newsletter subscribers. The infrastructure you have is sufficient — the routing from social to owned does not yet exist. Every week this is not in place is a week of audience built on borrowed infrastructure that can change its terms without notice.

Move two: build the written layer that converts existing production into a second distribution channel

You already dominate YouTube search for vibe coding terms. You have no presence in Google web search for the same queries. One companion article per YouTube video — not a transcript, but a structured written guide with key steps, code snippets, and tool context — indexed on an owned domain would begin capturing web search intent that currently flows to competitors and third-party sites. The content already exists in video form. The production constraint is editorial formatting, not ideation.

The rileybrown.ai domain — unregistered by you, directly on-brand, and matching your TikTok handle pattern — is the natural home for this. Matt Wolfe's futuretools.io demonstrates what a secondary written content property can do for an AI creator: it generates newsletter subscribers independent of YouTube and builds search authority that appreciates over time rather than depending on the weekly upload cycle. This position is currently undefended in your niche.

Move three: close the loop between the product and the channel

No creator in your peer set is also building the product their audience uses. VibeCode has 10,000 paid users and 40,000-plus generated apps. Your YouTube channel has 251,000 subscribers. These two assets are running in parallel rather than feeding each other. Closing the loop means adding visible "try what I'm building" context around VibeCode inside tutorials — framed as a creator demonstrating their own product, not as a sponsorship read — and adding a "learn from Riley" entry point inside the VibeCode onboarding flow. Two-way traffic between a funded startup and a growing YouTube channel is a flywheel no competitor can replicate.

If the Maven full course is live — it was planned for January 2026 and that date has passed — it should appear in your YouTube channel description as the primary educational deeplink. A course that cannot be found from its creator's own channel is not in market in any meaningful sense.

The positioning question underneath all three moves

"The first vibe coder" is working now. In 12 to 18 months it may not, because the term is on a commoditization curve: every AI tool company is adopting "vibe coding" language, and as the terminology diffuses across the industry, its differentiation value shrinks. The positioning that will survive this is not tool-centric ("I'll teach you to build without code") but outcome-and-identity-centric: "you don't need to become a developer to build real products."

The sharpest version of this — and the one only you can own — is that you are not just teaching vibe coding, you are building the infrastructure that vibe coders use. Peers teach with tools; you built one. That is a claim that connects directly to VibeCode, connects directly to every tutorial, and cannot be replicated by anyone in your peer set from where they stand today. It is worth making more loudly.
